Title: Phase Transitions
Date: 2010-12-03 10:20
Category: physics
Tags: statistics

Our planet is special from its pleasant atmosphere down to its churning iron core. Alhough these two features are on opposite extremes, they are both critical to maintaining life. Our active core protects the atmosphere by shielding it with a magnetic field, and the atmosphere keeps us at a reasonable temperature. This temperature allows for Earth's most glamorous of features: the sapphire seas that cover a vast majority of its surface. It simply cannot be overstated how important water is to life, and much can be said about the unique features that place it on such a high pedestal. However, it is not enough to simply have an abundance of H20. Without the high temperatures of our core, the magnetic field would weaken and allow the Sun to strip away our atmosphere. Without the temperature regulation admitted by our atmosphere, our water would evaporate or freeze. [This is the importance of phase transitions.]

The first phase transition described mathematically is due to Johannes Diderik van der Waals, whose surname is familiar to the seasoned students in physics and chemistry. Before we explain what it all means, let's see what the van der Waals model looks like.

\[P = RT/(v-b) - a/v^2\]
\[T = u/(cR) + a/(cRv)\]

Unless you are among the priviledged few, this symbols need description. The big T stands for temperature, whose importance has already been alluded to. The big P stands for pressure, and the little v basically stands for volume. Even if you haven't encountered the van der Waals equations, you may recall these symbols used in the ideal gas law.

\[PV = nRT\]

Our focus is mostly on pressure and temperature here; if you increase the temperature of a balloon, the gas will push harder against the latex. While visually simpler than the two equations shown earlier, it assumes that each particle in the balloon has no size. This simplification has little bearing on intuition, so van der Waals made it more realistic by adding the \[b\] parameter to account for the particle's size. The ideal gas law also doesn't account for electric charge or polarization. In the van der Waals equations, the parameter \[a\] accounts for the particle-to-particle attraction that follows. Try it out: if the particles have no size (\[b=0\]) and no attractive forces (\[a=0\]), we get back the ideal gas law! Great, so we made the model a bit more realistic. 




These geological mechanisms, paired with the diverse life that has sprouted within, balance to provide a suitable landscape for us to live.
