Counting to Infinity

tags: series

Is infinity foreign or familiar? As a concept, it evokes ideas of something boundless and eternal, almost dreamy in contrast to the transience of life. We wrap it neatly in a bow -- the iconic \(\infty\) -- and accept its mystic charm with little debate. Yet, behind this simple symbol, thousands of years of history stretch from recent decades to the very origins of human intelligence.

Bone and Clay

If you look around, everything can be described by mathematics in one way or another. The integers are often the most intuitive: this sentence has 3 numbers, 5 punctuation marks, 20 words… things to count on your fingers! In fact, the earliest evidence of mathematics is a simple set of tally marks etched in a quartz-tipped bone from over 20,000 years ago -- the Ishango bone from Central Africa. The number 3, for example, is found near the top in the form of three consecutive notches.

\begin{equation*} \textrm{III} \rightarrow 3 \end{equation*}

Despite the simplicity, there is a lot of power in this notation. It sets a singular unit that can be repeated to count as many things as you need. In practice, this was clearly satisfactory, because a period of 16,000 years passed before the next class of numbers appeared on record: the rational numbers. Dozens of these fractions take the stage on a Babylonian tablet, stamped purposefully across a flattened block of clay around 4,000 years ago and unceremoniously dubbed Plimpton 322 by its discoverers. The mathematical insight held in this artifact is far from obvious -- maybe a hundred people in the world can read the language with proficiency. Fortunately, like notches on a bone, some of this cuneiform is easy enough to transcribe with a bit of knowledge of numeral systems. Most modern counting uses base-10: we can count ten things with our fingers before a tally needs to be marked elsewhere. On the other hand, consider the tick of clock: after 59 seconds, we add a minute and start back at zero. This is the base-60 system used by the mathematicians of ancient Babylon. Let's take a look at the fifth row of script on Plimpton 322 as an example.

\begin{equation*} 𒐕\ 𒐏𒐜\ 𒐐𒐘\ 𒐕\ 𒐏\ \rightarrow \ \frac{1}{60^0} + \frac{48}{60^1} + \frac{54}{60^2} + \frac{1}{60^3} +\frac{40}{60^4} = \frac{97^2}{72^2} \end{equation*}
\begin{equation*} \hspace{-3.3cm} 𒐕\ 𒐐\ \hspace{4.3em} \rightarrow \ \frac{1}{60^0} + \frac{5}{60^1} = \frac{65}{60} \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \end{equation*}
\begin{equation*} \hspace{-4.3cm} 𒐕\ 𒌍𒐛\ \hspace{3em} \rightarrow \ \frac{1}{60^0} + \frac{37}{60^1} = \frac{97}{60} \end{equation*}

If you count each grouping of symbols like a tally, you might recognize them as the numerators in our translation. If you look even closer, you'll find the famous theorem of Pythagoras scribed a millenium before he was born! Illustrated here by 65² + 72² = 97², each line on the tablet represents the sides of a triangle. Although their notation seems distant from the fractions we use today, its application to geometry reveals our first hint of infinity.

The first boundless mathematical form was an irrational number. Rather than being infinitely large, however, these numbers are infinitely long. For motivation, consider a triangle whose height is equal to its width.

Another Babylonian tablet -- pressed in the same millennium and titled YBC 7289 in a similarly unsavory fashion -- takes the first recorded step towards irrational numbers. This round chunk of clay holds the first known approximation of the square root of two, whose fractional representation is now known to be infinite in extent.

\begin{equation*} \hspace{-0.1cm} 𒐕\ ⟪𒐘\ 𒐐𒐕\ 𒌋\ \hspace{1.6em} \rightarrow \ \frac{1}{60^0} + \frac{24}{60^1} + \frac{51}{60^2} + \frac{10}{60^3} = 1.41421\overline{296} \end{equation*}

After another millennium, this strange number became the bane of the Pythagorean cult of saintly geeks. They considered the simplest nontrivial case of his theorem: a triangle with perpendicular sides of unit length. To their grief, there is neither an integer nor a fraction satisfying the equation 1² + 1² = 𝑥². This was shown rigorously by the great Euclid, whose detailed proof of the Pythagorean theorem remains the earliest in recorded history. His concrete revelation of an endless number — especially as it arises in the simplest of geometries — provoked fiery philosophical and mathematical debate. Motivated by this challenge, Zeno of Elea took the stage a couple of centuries later. This master of paradoxes introduced a tangible yet enigmatic approach to endlessness in terms of an infinite series of fractions. He pondered a simple problem: if every step traveled is half of the distance towards your destination, you would need an infinite number of steps to reach it. This is best illustrated schematically by the splitting of a square.

Zeno's infinite series paradox is intriguing even to the modern scholar. Although devised in terms of fractions, it is countable in essence: you can easily tally every step as an etching on a bone or a stamp in soft clay. And yet, far before reaching your destination, the bone would be chiseled to dust, the clay rendered illegible. Fortunately, we have learned how to deal with this complication — the seasoned reader might recall the convergence of the infinite geometric series. Even so, it was not for another millennium that infinity was granted its own symbol, appropriately scribed as \(\infty\) by an English cryptographer, John Wallis. Through our adoption of this mark, we can refer to an endless infinity with a simple squiggle on paper instead of an impossibly etched bone, yet this gloss only masks its elusive and problematic air. To wit, infinity has never appeared in any physical measurement or observation, and we still struggle to handle its immensity in theory. Methods of truncating it to something manageable have been introduced — Cauchy's principle value is a notable example that remains critical in quantum electrodynamics — but those are complicated stories for another time. For now, you may bask in its incommensurate complexity and appreciate the millennia it took to define. Else, as you count sheep in bed, perhaps you will feel a new solace in knowing you will fall asleep long before infinity is reached.

Further Considerations

What we have discussed here may be categorized as explicit counting, as opposed to proto-numerical cognition. The former is deliberate and formulaic, like etchings on a bone that can be abstracted to track the phases of the moon or count prime numbers. The latter is innate and passes like wind through the mind. For example, when the average guppy is faced with two shoals — one with 3 fish and another with 10 — it will tend to join the larger of the two. Though this distinction verges on the philosophical, the guppy's analysis is arguably distinct from mathematical reasoning. Sure, an aggregate observation can be developed into something more advanced, but it is more difficult to differentiate 100 fish from 101 than it is to see that 3 fish is fewer than 10. There may not be much benefit in nature for the guppy to have such fine-grained counting abilities. On the other hand, you would likely not give someone 101 apples in exchange for 100 oranges. Metaphors aside, the point is that humans developed the means and necessity to push numerical analysis from subconscious observations to explicit constructions. How, why, and when this occured is outside of the scope of my expertise, but the gap even between ourselves and other great apes in this regard is immense. As a closing remark, we only know what has remained over time. It seems much more likely that counting originated through means that did not last the test of time. A meticulously counted stack of stones will be inevitably lost with the autumn breeze, but a well-preserved bone is practically eternal.
